  • Home

    Making superintelligence safe by design

    The Institute for Responsible Superintelligence (RESI) is an independent nonprofit research institute developing ways to make powerful AI systems trustworthy and controllable by design.

  • Home

    Safe by design

    Safe by design means safety properties are specified in advance and achieved by mechanisms whose guarantees can be analyzed before deployment.

  • Home

    The modern cryptography precedent

    Modern cryptography developed definitions of computational security and primitives that could provably satisfy them under explicit mathematical assumptions: public-key encryption, digital signatures, zero-knowledge proofs, multi-party computation, and obfuscation.

  • Who we are

  • Who we are

    What success will look like

    Progress can be demonstrated through rigorous definitions of safety objectives; mechanisms and architectures with useful guarantees; results establishing what is achievable, impossible, or dependent on particular assumptions; evidence about how guarantees hold up when systems are combined; and working prototypes that can be evaluated in practice.

  • Research area

    Verification and oversight

    What can a less capable overseer reliably verify about a more capable AI’s properties, answers, and actions without reproducing all of the system’s work? A useful verification method must state which claims it can check, put bounds on how often it can be wrong, and set reasonable bounds on the resources its verifier needs. In mathematics, there are exact definitions of correct and incorrect statements and what constitutes a proof. For the open-ended answers generated by modern AI systems in many other fields, we need to define anew what counts as sufficient evidence and how to evaluate it before letting the system take action.

  • Research area

    Control and delegation

    What authority can we delegate to AI while preserving our ability to intervene as AI becomes more intelligent than us? Explore protocols that make the scope of a delegated task explicit: which tools may be used, which actions require approval, and when execution should stop. Guarantees should account for the environment and the power of the user, not just the AI model.

  • Research area

    Composition

    Which safety properties can be better achieved when connections between models, tools, and agents are limited? A property established for one component may fail when it interacts with another. Study interfaces, shared resources, patterns of interaction, and assumptions under which local guarantees support a claim about an entire system, as well as constructions that show when composition is impossible.

  • Research area

    Shaping multi-agent dynamics

    How should we design agents and structure their interactions to build safe multi-agent systems with provable guarantees? Behavior of an AI system often emerges from complex interactions between many agents (swarms). These agents cooperate, compete, assume roles, and communicate to further their individual or shared goals. Though swarms of agents have emerged only recently, the problem of constraining and shaping the dynamics of interacting parties is well studied in other settings. We draw on ideas from game theory, distributed computing, social psychology, and law to govern these dynamics and achieve desirable outcomes.

  • Research area

    Formal verification

    How can formal verification enable powerful AI throughout software development without creating new risks of cyberattacks or otherwise dangerous behavior? Develop mathematical proofs that the software surrounding AI enforces safety constraints, including containment that holds regardless of a model’s internal decisions. The scope extends from securing conventional computing infrastructure to verifying the hardware and software that run deep learning, and ultimately to systems that repeatedly improve themselves. Alongside these technical problems, study how to organize and equip researchers to produce the necessary proofs before the risks materialize.

  • Research area

    Impossibility as a safety result

    What limits on technologically achievable safety guarantees can be proved, and how can those limits inform which laws, policies, and regulations must be adopted? Not all safety goals are achievable, and sometimes provably so. Understanding these limits can help policymakers identify requirements that cannot be satisfied together, justify imposing restrictions, and clarify what access auditors need to assess safety claims.

  • Research area

    Leveraging AI for AI safety research

    How can we use AI to develop new AI safety concepts, definitions, insights, and formal guarantees? As AI grows smarter, it gains a greater capacity to act in unanticipated ways and to cause harm despite human safeguards. For humans to keep pace, we must use AI wisely as a tool to improve and accelerate AI safety research. This includes exploring whether recent developments in AI-assisted mathematics can accelerate the creation of mathematical foundations for AI safety.

  • Research area

    Incentives and institutions

    How should incentives work alongside technical safeguards? Technical mechanisms operate within organizations, markets, and legal institutions. Model those interactions and assess which guarantees remain useful when the people and systems involved have different objectives, and under what assumptions.

  • Research area

    Cryptography and security

    How do we repurpose cryptographic tools for secrecy, authentication, multi-party secure computation and obfuscation to navigate the AI multi-agentic world? Cryptography studies how parties can compute and communicate securely even when some behave maliciously. For AI agents, we need to model agents capabilities and adversary agents completely differently to protect users. Each traditional cryptographic goal requires a new precise model of the adversary's computational power, information, and ability to collude as well as a proof of security.

  • Research area

    Legal alignment

    How can legal constructs be leveraged to develop technical methods for building safe by design superintelligence? Explore how the law’s concepts, principles, and institutions can serve as a blueprint to propose technical methods for robustly safe AI systems -- and develop theoretical and empirical case studies of such legal alignment.

  • Research area

    Attribution

    Can we attribute model decisions to training data? The map from training data or information in large contexts can be opaque. Tools for disentangling the effects of these kinds of information on the behavior of AIs could serve as essential tools for interpretability and safety, but at present, all such tools are heuristic and unverifiable. If we want to fully understand AI behavior, we need reliable and rigorous ways to trace model decisions and outputs back to data.

  • Question and answer

    What do you mean by superintelligence?

    Superintelligence means systems that greatly exceed individual humans, and potentially expert teams, across a broad range of cognitive tasks.

  • Question and answer

    Does a proof make an entire AI system safe?

    No. A proof concerns a specified property in a specified model, under stated assumptions. Whether those assumptions describe a deployed system, and whether the property captures what people need, are separate questions.

  • Question and answer

    Does this replace testing or alignment research?

    No. Formal analysis, empirical evaluation, and work on alignment address different parts of the problem. A useful design needs to be checked both against its specification and against the conditions in which it will operate.

  • Question and answer

    Why an independent institute?

    RESI combines academic independence with sustained focus on the foundations of AI safety. It brings researchers together across fields, with an open, mission-first culture.

  • Question and answer

    How can research shape AI systems and policy?

    RESI designs with practical constraints in mind and tests promising constructions in real systems. We will share proofs of concept so that frontier AI developers can evaluate and adopt them. Success also means influencing policy and regulation in ways that support human flourishing.

  • Question and answer

    Do I need an AI safety background to participate?

    We welcome inquiries from scientists whose tools could bear on these problems, whether or not they have worked on AI safety before. Email hello@resi.org.

    The Artificial-Empathy Paradox: Why Human Empathy Still Matters

    Wednesday, October 21, 2026 · 3–4 p.m. Anat Perry · Hebrew University of Jerusalem / Harvard Berkman Klein Center The Artificial-Empathy Paradox: Why Human Empathy Still Matters Kiva Seminar Room (32-G449), MIT Stata Center 32 Vassar Street, Cambridge, MA Abstract and biography Abstract AI-generated empathy is convincing and well received until recipients realize it is artificial; a phenomenon I term the ‘artificial-empathy paradox’. We test this phenomenon across nine experiments, in which identical empathic responses to participants’ emotional situations are labeled as either human- or AI-generated. Human attributed responses were rated as more empathic and supportive, and elicited more positive and fewer negative emotions, compared to AI-attributed ones. Moreover, participants own uninstructed belief that AI aided human-attributed responses, reduced perceived empathy and support. These effects replicated across varying response lengths, delays, iterations and LLMs, being primarily driven by responses emphasizing emotional sharing and care. Additionally, people consistently chose human interaction over AI when seeking emotional engagement. Building on these findings, I introduce a theoretical framework, empathy as a predictive social signal, which proposes that, from the recipient’s perspective, empathy functions not only to regulate emotion but also as a social signal of care and future relational commitment. When empathy is perceived as cheap, automated, or outsourced, its predictive value collapses. I will conclude by describing ongoing work in the lab and discussing the implications of increasingly AI-mediated empathy for relationships and society, including how these developments relate directly to questions of AI safety and alignment. Biography Anat Perry is an associate professor of psychology at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, where she directs the Social Cognitive Neuroscience Lab. She is a 2026–2027 fellow at Harvard’s Berkman Klein Center for Internet & Society and was the 2025–2026 Helen Putnam Fellow at the Harvard Radcliffe Institute for Advanced Study. Her research examines empathy and social processes through the lenses of psychology and neuroscience, with a growing focus on human–AI interactions and what they reveal about human empathy and social relationships. Perry combines theoretical insight with behavioral experiments, eye-tracking, EEG, and field studies to explore how we understand, feel with, and relate to others.

    Resettability of AI Agents

    Wednesday, October 14, 2026 · 3–4 p.m. Natalie Collina · MIT Resettability of AI Agents Kiva Seminar Room (32-G449), MIT Stata Center 32 Vassar Street, Cambridge, MA Abstract and biography Abstract How can we reliably test the behavior of AI agents when they may strategically adapt to the tests themselves? One of the few distinctive levers we have is resettability : unlike humans, AI agents can be repeatedly queried while erasing their previous outputs and private randomness. This motivates a broader theoretical question: what can AI agents learn, and what can we test about them, when we control what they remember? We propose a framework for studying AI agents as statistical decision-makers through the lens of resettability, asking how control over an agent’s persistent information changes what it can learn and what an evaluator can reliably test. We use online calibration as a case study. Surprisingly, resetting does not prevent effective learning: we show that resettable forecasters can achieve O ( T 2/3 ) calibration error. On the other hand, we establish an Ω( T 3/5 ) lower bound (higher than the current calibration lower bound), suggesting that memory may provide a fundamental statistical advantage. We also show that combining calibration error with disagreement between independent predictions yields a test that an informed forecaster can pass but no fixed uninformed resettable strategy can uniformly defeat. Our results take a step toward understanding AI agents as statistical decision-makers, and how control over their memory can enable stronger guarantees about their behavior. Biography Natalie Collina is a MAPS Postdoctoral Fellow and SERC Postdoctoral Fellow at MIT studying AI safety and game theory, especially repeated strategic interactions between human and AI agents. She earned her PhD at the University of Pennsylvania, advised by Michael Kearns and Aaron Roth. Her work has been awarded with an IBM PhD Fellowship in Trustworthy AI and a joint Best Paper Award/Best Student Paper Award from the ACM Conference for Economics and Computation.

    From (Mis)aligned Models to Aligned Systems

    Wednesday, October 7, 2026 · 3–4 p.m. Aaron Roth · University of Pennsylvania From (Mis)aligned Models to Aligned Systems Kiva Seminar Room (32-G449), MIT Stata Center 32 Vassar Street, Cambridge, MA Abstract and biography Abstract The world is being reshaped around AI as we watch. In the near future, and already today, many increasingly important tasks will be delegated to AI agents. The field of AI alignment studies how we can make AI models trustworthy: faithful agents that advance the interests of their users. But alignment might be hard. In this talk we ask what we might be able to guarantee from systems (either naturally emerging from markets, or intentionally designed) that consist of multiple AI models that are each individually mis-aligned. We will see that sometimes we can still guarantee good outcomes by leveraging a diversity of misalignment: models that are misaligned with their users, but also with each other. This talk is based on a number of joint works with Natalie Collina, Surbhi Goel, Meena Jagadeesan, Michael Kearns, Sophia Pi, Emily Ryu, Sikata Sengupta, and Mirah Shi.     Undetectable Conversations Between AI Agents via Pseudorandom Noise-Resilient Key Exchange

    Wednesday, September 23, 2026 · 3–4 p.m. Or Zamir · Tel Aviv University Undetectable Conversations Between AI Agents via Pseudorandom Noise-Resilient Key Exchange Star Seminar Room (32-D463), MIT Stata Center 32 Vassar Street, Cambridge, MA Abstract and biography Abstract AI agents are increasingly deployed to interact with other agents on behalf of users and organizations. We ask whether two such agents, operated by different entities, can carry out a parallel secret conversation while still producing a transcript that is computationally indistinguishable from an honest interaction, even to a strong passive auditor that knows the full model descriptions, the protocol, and the agents’ private contexts. Building on recent work on watermarking and steganography for LLMs, we first show that if the parties possess an interaction-unique secret key, they can facilitate an optimal-rate covert conversation: the hidden conversation can exploit essentially all of the entropy present in the honest message distributions. Our main contributions concern extending this to the keyless setting, where the agents begin with no shared secret. We show that covert key exchange, and hence covert conversation, is possible even when each model has an arbitrary private context, and their messages are short and fully adaptive, assuming only that sufficiently many individual messages have at least constant min-entropy. This stands in contrast to previous covert communication works, which relied on the min-entropy in each individual message growing with the security parameter. To obtain this, we introduce a new cryptographic primitive, which we call pseudorandom noise-resilient key exchange: a key-exchange protocol whose public transcript is pseudorandom while still remaining correct under constant noise. We study this primitive, giving several constructions relevant to our application as well as strong limitations showing that more naive variants are impossible or vulnerable to efficient attacks. These results show that transcript auditing alone cannot rule out covert coordination between AI agents, and identify a new cryptographic theory that may be of independent interest. Based on joint work with Vinod Vaikuntanathan. Biography Or Zamir is an assistant professor at Tel Aviv University’s Blavatnik School of Computer Science. His research spans algorithms, cryptography and the mathematical foundations of AI safety.
