How can we formally define and implement AI safety?

RESI focuses on the formal and design-oriented foundations of AI safety: precise safety objectives, explicit assumptions, mechanisms with analyzable guarantees, composition, and impossibility results.

Guarantees depend on stated assumptions about the system and adversary. They do not establish the safety of an entire AI system.

Our goal is to build approaches that develop together with systems as they become superintelligent.

Research at RESI is AI-assisted from the start, with AI-generated suggestions subject to independent examination and testing. We will develop and continually improve tools and workflows that use frontier AI agents at every stage, expanding the ideas and approaches our team can investigate.

Research outputs

Across our research areas, we pursue the following outputs:

  • Models, definitions, and impossibility results

    Develop models and precise definitions for AI safety.

    Establish where guarantees are impossible under stated assumptions.

  • Mechanisms and composition

    Develop mechanisms, protocols, and architectures that provide useful guarantees, and study whether those guarantees survive composition into larger systems involving models, tools, people, and institutions.

  • Implementations and proofs of concept

    Build implementations and proofs of concept. Where implementation requires resources beyond RESI’s capacity, share proofs of concept that frontier AI developers can evaluate and adopt.

Definitions and constructions develop together: a new protocol may reveal the right concept, and an impossibility result may show that a problem must be reformulated.

Research areas

These questions describe our key focus areas. In each area we pursue clear outputs that include definitions, mechanisms, working demonstrations, and results on what can and cannot be guaranteed.

Verification and oversight

What can a less capable overseer reliably verify about a more capable AI’s properties, answers, and actions without reproducing all of the system’s work?

A useful verification method must state which claims it can check, put bounds on how often it can be wrong, and set reasonable bounds on the resources its verifier needs. In mathematics, there are exact definitions of correct and incorrect statements and what constitutes a proof. For the open-ended answers generated by modern AI systems in many other fields, we need to define anew what counts as sufficient evidence and how to evaluate it before letting the system take action.

We collaborate on this area through the Cryptographic verification working group, led by Shafi Goldwasser.

Control and delegation

What authority can we delegate to AI while preserving our ability to intervene as AI becomes more intelligent than us?

Explore protocols that make the scope of a delegated task explicit: which tools may be used, which actions require approval, and when execution should stop. Guarantees should account for the environment and the power of the user, not just the AI model.

We collaborate on this area through the Monitoring and control working group, led by Vinod Vaikuntanathan.

Composition

Which safety properties can be better achieved when connections between models, tools, and agents are limited?

A property established for one component may fail when it interacts with another. Study interfaces, shared resources, patterns of interaction, and assumptions under which local guarantees support a claim about an entire system, as well as constructions that show when composition is impossible.

We collaborate on this area through the Modular architectures working group, led by Adam Tauman Kalai.

Shaping multi-agent dynamics

How should we design agents and structure their interactions to build safe multi-agent systems with provable guarantees?

Behavior of an AI system often emerges from complex interactions between many agents (swarms). These agents cooperate, compete, assume roles, and communicate to further their individual or shared goals. Though swarms of agents have emerged only recently, the problem of constraining and shaping the dynamics of interacting parties is well studied in other settings. We draw on ideas from game theory, distributed computing, social psychology, and law to govern these dynamics and achieve desirable outcomes.

We collaborate on this area through the Game-theoretic foundations of AI safety working group, led by Yannai A. Gonczarowski.

Formal verification

How can formal verification enable powerful AI throughout software development without creating new risks of cyberattacks or otherwise dangerous behavior?

Develop mathematical proofs that the software surrounding AI enforces safety constraints, including containment that holds regardless of a model’s internal decisions. The scope extends from securing conventional computing infrastructure to verifying the hardware and software that run deep learning, and ultimately to systems that repeatedly improve themselves. Alongside these technical problems, study how to organize and equip researchers to produce the necessary proofs before the risks materialize.

We collaborate on this area through the Formal verification working group, led by Adam Chlipala.

Impossibility as a safety result

What limits on technologically achievable safety guarantees can be proved, and how can those limits inform which laws, policies, and regulations must be adopted?

Not all safety goals are achievable, and sometimes provably so. Understanding these limits can help policymakers identify requirements that cannot be satisfied together, justify imposing restrictions, and clarify what access auditors need to assess safety claims.

Leveraging AI for AI safety research

How can we use AI to develop new AI safety concepts, definitions, insights, and formal guarantees?

As AI grows smarter, it gains a greater capacity to act in unanticipated ways and to cause harm despite human safeguards. For humans to keep pace, we must use AI wisely as a tool to improve and accelerate AI safety research. This includes exploring whether recent developments in AI-assisted mathematics can accelerate the creation of mathematical foundations for AI safety.

We collaborate on this area through the AI harnesses working group, led by Jonathan Shafer.

Incentives and institutions

How should incentives work alongside technical safeguards?

Technical mechanisms operate within organizations, markets, and legal institutions. Model those interactions and assess which guarantees remain useful when the people and systems involved have different objectives, and under what assumptions.

We collaborate on this area through the Game-theoretic foundations of AI safety working group, led by Yannai A. Gonczarowski.

Cryptography and security

How do we repurpose cryptographic tools for secrecy, authentication, multi-party secure computation and obfuscation to navigate the AI multi-agentic world?

Cryptography studies how parties can compute and communicate securely even when some behave maliciously. For AI agents, we need to model agents capabilities and adversary agents completely differently to protect users. Each traditional cryptographic goal requires a new precise model of the adversary's computational power, information, and ability to collude as well as a proof of security.

We collaborate on this area through the Cryptography and cybersecurity working group, led by Yaron Singer.

Legal alignment

How can legal constructs be leveraged to develop technical methods for building safe by design superintelligence?

Explore how the law’s concepts, principles, and institutions can serve as a blueprint to propose technical methods for robustly safe AI systems -- and develop theoretical and empirical case studies of such legal alignment.

We collaborate on this area through the Legal alignment working group, led by Noam Kolt.

Attribution

Can we attribute model decisions to training data?

The map from training data or information in large contexts can be opaque. Tools for disentangling the effects of these kinds of information on the behavior of AIs could serve as essential tools for interpretability and safety, but at present, all such tools are heuristic and unverifiable. If we want to fully understand AI behavior, we need reliable and rigorous ways to trace model decisions and outputs back to data.

We collaborate on this area through the Data attribution working group, led by Sam Gunn.

Working groups

Working groups bring RESI researchers together to develop research outputsacross our key research areas. They open new directions and revisit classical questions in light of superintelligence. Each group is organized by one or more leaders who decide on its structure. Alongside working groups, RESI co-hosts seminars with MIT on the foundations of AI safety.

    Nature · 2026
    Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh S. Vempala, Edwin Zhang
    Why language models hallucinateWhy do models guess instead of admitting uncertainty? This work analyzes how training and evaluation reward guessing, and argues for scoring systems that make acknowledging uncertainty a better strategy.Changing the scoring rule is not a guarantee that hallucinations disappear.
  • Models That Prove Their Own Correctness
    NeurIPS · 2025
    Noga Amit, Shafi Goldwasser, Orr Paradise, Guy N. Rothblum
    The NeurIPS 2025 version appeared as “A Theory for Worst-Case vs. Average-Case Guarantees for LLMs.”
    Models that can prove their answersA framework for learning models that supply interactive proofs of their outputs. Demonstrated on greatest-common-divisor computation, not unrestricted natural-language answers.
  • How to Avoid Debate: Scalable AI Safety via Doubly-Efficient Interactive Proofs
    ICML · 2026
    L. Chen, Y. Tauman Kalai, Z. Xi
    Develops methods for checking certain computations that use external information, such as database records or human judgments. The guarantees require specific structure in that information or computations that tolerate a limited number of errors.
  • BarrierNet: Differentiable Control Barrier Functions for Learning of Safe Robot Control
    IEEE Transactions on Robotics · 2023
    W. Xiao, T.-H. Wang, R. Hasani, M. Chahine, A. Amini, X. Li, D. Rus
    Introduces a trainable safety layer that constrains a robot’s actions to satisfy specified requirements, with guarantees that depend on the model of the robot and its environment.
  • Consensus Sampling for Safer Generative AI
    Presented at IASEAI · 2026
    A. Kalai, Y. Tauman Kalai, O. Zamir
    Selects an output from several models, or declines to answer, with a provable limit on the risk of harmful output relative to the more reliable models. The method requires access to each model’s output probabilities. Producing useful answers also depends on sufficient overlap among the reliable models’ output distributions.
  • Governing AI Agents
    Notre Dame Law Review · 2026
    N. Kolt
    Examines the responsibilities and limits of authority that arise when AI agents act for people, and explains why existing approaches to monitoring and accountability may be insufficient.
  • On the Impossibility of Separating Intelligence from Judgment: The Computational Intractability of Filtering for AI Alignment
    ICLR · 2026
    S. Ball, G. Gluch, S. Goldwasser, F. Kreuter, O. Reingold, G. Rothblum
    Understanding the limits of filteringCryptographic constructions expose computational limits of certain input and output filters. The results depend on explicit hardness assumptions and formal settings.Establishes computational barriers to external prompt and output filtering under cryptographic hardness assumptions and specified relationships between the model and filter. These worst-case results do not say that filtering has no practical value.
  • Planting Undetectable Backdoors in Machine Learning Models
    FOCS · 2022
    S. Goldwasser, M. Kim, V. Vaikuntanathan, O. Zamir
    Shows how a model can appear to function normally while containing a hidden trigger that changes its behavior. Under cryptographic assumptions, such backdoors can evade inspection within feasible computing limits.
  • A Cryptographic Perspective on Mitigation vs. Detection in Machine Learning
    Preprint · 2025
    G. Gluch, S. Goldwasser
    Shows that, for certain tasks involving generated outputs, a defense can limit harmful behavior even when the harmful inputs cannot be efficiently identified.
  • Pseudorandom Error-Correcting Codes
    CRYPTO · 2024
    M. Christ, S. Gunn

  • Oblivious Defense in ML Models: Backdoor Removal without Detection

  • Algorithmic Collusion by Large Language Models

    S. Fish, Y. A. Gonczarowski, R. I. Shorrer

  • Superintelligence and Law

    TMLR · 2026
